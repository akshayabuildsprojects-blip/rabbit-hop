## 1. Product discovery channels and shopping entry points

- **[Evidence]** Discovery is multi-touchpoint rather than single-channel: physical retail is the most common source of beauty inspiration, followed by friends and family and then online retail, with social and brand marketing also contributing. ([The future of the beauty industry in 2025 and beyond | McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/consumer-packaged-goods/our-insights/a-close-look-at-the-global-beauty-industry-in-2025)) 
- **[Evidence]** Search behaves as a relay: Google is the first stop for 43% of beauty consumers, while 57% start elsewhere across Instagram (11.9%), YouTube (11.2%), TikTok (10.6%) and AI tools (9.8%); when a product is found on TikTok, 72% next search for it on Google and only 7% buy directly through TikTok at that moment. ([57% of beauty consumers don’t start with Google Search](https://searchengineland.com/beauty-consumers-google-search-493828)) 
- **[Evidence]** Digital is routinely involved even when purchase finishes elsewhere: more than half of cosmetics and perfume purchases involve an online touchpoint, and shoppers who browse across multiple retailers discover more brands than single-retailer shoppers. ([New Health & Beauty research: 5 shopper trends every marketer should know | Criteo](https://www.criteo.com/blog/5-beauty-shopper-trends-2026/)) 
- **[Evidence]** Nearly half of surveyed beauty consumers (47.7%) say they typically complete beauty purchases on Amazon, illustrating that discovery, validation and conversion often occur in different places. ([57% of beauty consumers don’t start with Google Search](https://searchengineland.com/beauty-consumers-google-search-493828)) 
- **[Assumption]** For this archetype, entry is typically need- or replenishment-led (running low, need faster morning routine) rather than trend-led browsing, so discovery is more likely to start from a remembered product, in-store scan, or targeted search than from open-ended social exploration.

## 2. Influence of social media, creators, trends and social proof

- **[Evidence]** Social influence on purchase is widespread but not youth-only: 41% of all respondents made a beauty purchase in the last six months based on something seen on TikTok or YouTube, rising to 60% in their 30s and still 34% in their 50s cohort. ([57% of beauty consumers don’t start with Google Search](https://searchengineland.com/beauty-consumers-google-search-493828)) 
- **[Evidence]** Creators drive discovery more than final decision: only 2% name influencer recommendations as a driver when deciding whether to buy a new product, while influencers remain key for product discovery; price (72%) and effectiveness (55%) dominate the buying decision. ([Is Your Brand Really Worth It? Beauty Shoppers Want Proof, New Report Finds](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/is-your-brand-really-worth-it-beauty-shoppers-want-proof-new-report-finds-302921803.html)) 
- **[Evidence]** Customer reviews slightly outweigh creator videos at conversion: 42% cite regular customer reviews/comments as a factor in online beauty buying versus 41% citing influencer/celebrity video reviews, with creators described as scouts and reviews as fact-checkers. ([Who’s Really Influencing the Way You Shop for Beauty?](https://en.redtram.com/lifestyle/623973004/)) 
- **[Evidence]** Trust varies by format: YouTube creators are rated “very trustworthy” by 14.1% versus 8.6% for TikTok creators; YouTube leads for tutorials where detail matters, while TikTok is near-competitive with Google only for dupes/alternatives. ([57% of beauty consumers don’t start with Google Search](https://searchengineland.com/beauty-consumers-google-search-493828)) 
- **[Assumption]** This archetype filters social content for practical utility — wear-time demos, application difficulty, routine simplification — rather than virality or novelty, which distinguishes it from the Trend-Driven Beauty Shopper where staying current itself is rewarding.
- **[Assumption]** Trend sensitivity is low-to-moderate and conditional: a viral product only enters the shortlist if reviews and demonstrations suggest it saves steps or performs reliably; otherwise it is ignored.

## 3. Product evaluation and comparison habits

- **[Evidence]** Evaluation is research-intensive for many beauty shoppers: U.S. shoppers browse an average of 19 makeup products and 12 fragrances before purchase, and globally reviews/ratings are the top purchase influence with nearly half relying on them. ([New Health & Beauty research: 5 shopper trends every marketer should know | Criteo](https://www.criteo.com/blog/5-beauty-shopper-trends-2026/)) 
- **[Evidence]** When overwhelmed, shoppers use shortcuts to eliminate options: among skincare shoppers who feel overwhelmed, 50% say familiar brands help them decide, 48% turn to customer reviews/ratings, and 25% focus on visual appeal, with fewer relying on advertising. ([The Changing Face of Skincare Loyalty](https://www.happi.com/exclusives/the-changing-face-of-skincare-loyalty/)) 
- **[Evidence]** Effectiveness dominates evaluation criteria: 77% cite product effectiveness as key, with customer reviews and ingredients tied at 53.4%, dermatologist/aesthetician recommendations at 52.8%, and brand reputation at 52.7%. ([5 Factors Influencing Readers' Shopping Decisions](https://www.newbeauty.com/view/factors-influencing-readers-skin-care-shopping-decisions)) 
- **[Assumption]** Consistent with limited time and decision fatigue, this archetype researches selectively: checks a small shortlist (often 2–4 familiar or highly-rated options) on longevity, ease of application and fit for routine, then stops once one option meets a “reliable and easy enough” threshold rather than seeking the optimal product.
- **[Assumption]** Comparison depth is deliberately capped: detailed ingredient-by-ingredient or 10+-review deep dives are the exception; skimming ratings distribution, negative reviews about fading/creasing/difficult application, and professional endorsements is sufficient to decide.

## 4. Price sensitivity and perceived value

- **[Evidence]** Convenience commands a stated premium for many but not all: over 60% of U.S. consumers say they will pay more for things that make life easier and 42% prioritise convenience over price, especially younger consumers and parents with busier routines. ([Convenience as the New Consumer Currency](https://www.mintel.com/insights/consumer-research/convenience-as-the-new-consumer-currency/)) 
- **[Evidence]** The premium has a clear limit: 61% of U.S. online shoppers say lower cost matters more than convenience, and 90% would accept slower shipping for savings; delivery cost (36%) outranks speed (23%) and convenience (17%) as most important delivery aspect. ([Consumers still value convenience—but not at any price](https://www.emarketer.com/content/consumers-still-value-convenience-but-not-any-price)) 
- **[Evidence]** Proven performance justifies a premium more reliably than convenience alone: one-third of beauty consumers will pay 20% or more for skincare with proven efficacy credentials, and quality remains a stronger driver of premium spending than ease alone. ([Efficacy Overtakes Novelty as Key Driver of Ingredient Value: Euromonitor International](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20261102856593/en/Efficacy-Overtakes-Novelty-as-Key-Driver-of-Ingredient-Value-Euromonitor-International)) ; ([Convenience as the New Consumer Currency](https://www.mintel.com/insights/consumer-research/convenience-as-the-new-consumer-currency/)) 
- **[Evidence]** In Asia-Pacific beauty, 31% will pay 10–20% more for new/innovative formats, while 32.1% would rather buy fewer but higher-quality items, supporting a “pay for fewer, better, easier” logic rather than paying more for prestige. ([The Art of Effortless: Simplifying Your Beauty Game](https://WWW.EUROMONITOR.COM/article/the-art-of-effortless-simplifying-your-beauty-game)) ; 
- **[Assumption]** Tradeoff rule (illustrative, not a finding): she pays more when the premium buys verified time savings or reliable wear (for example, Scenario A: $45 long-wear foundation versus $20 requiring touch-ups, or Scenario E: premium with strong reliability reviews versus inconsistent cheap option) but chooses the cheaper acceptable option when performance evidence is equivalent — distinct from the Price-Sensitive Value Shopper who minimizes cost first and from the Luxury Shopper who values prestige/exclusivity.

## 5. Brand loyalty versus willingness to experiment

- **[Evidence]** Loyalty is fragile overall: only 34% say they rarely switch from preferred beauty brands, and up to 53% of global purchases across fragrance, skincare and makeup come from brands not purchased in the prior 12 months. ([New Health & Beauty research: 5 shopper trends every marketer should know | Criteo](https://www.criteo.com/blog/5-beauty-shopper-trends-2026/)) 
- **[Evidence]** Where loyalty exists in skincare it is performance-rooted, not emotional: 55% say quality most influences loyalty, with value second at 30%; nearly half would stop buying after a decline in quality and one-third after price increases. ([The Changing Face of Skincare Loyalty](https://www.happi.com/exclusives/the-changing-face-of-skincare-loyalty/)) ; 
- **[Evidence]** Switching is driven by perceived better results: nearly half of recent skincare purchasers cite product performance as primary reason to switch and 30% cite ingredients, with reviews, availability and reputation also influential. ([The Changing Face of Skincare Loyalty](https://www.happi.com/exclusives/the-changing-face-of-skincare-loyalty/)) 
- **[Assumption]** For this archetype, repurchase is habitual because it saves effort: once a product proves consistent, she defaults to it to avoid re-research, and only experiments when a new launch promises a clear, credible performance or time advantage (Scenario D: familiar trusted product versus new launch) — unlike the Trend-Driven shopper who experiments for novelty and unlike the Sensitive-Skin shopper who requires exhaustive suitability checks before any switch.
- **[Assumption]** Novelty seeking is low: unfamiliar products that add learning cost, uncertain shade match, or complicated application are rejected unless the practical gain is large and well-evidenced.

## 6. Trust in influencers, experts, communities and independent reviews

- **[Evidence]** Independent and professional voices outweigh influencers at decision: effectiveness (77%), reviews (53.4%), ingredients (53.4%), dermatologist/aesthetician recommendations (52.8%) and brand reputation (52.7%) all far exceed influencer/celebrity recommendations (10.8%). ([5 Factors Influencing Readers' Shopping Decisions](https://www.newbeauty.com/view/factors-influencing-readers-skin-care-shopping-decisions)) 
- **[Evidence]** Reviews are the single top global influence (nearly half rely on them), alongside friends/family, in-store testing and search engines for reassurance. ([New Health & Beauty research: 5 shopper trends every marketer should know | Criteo](https://www.criteo.com/blog/5-beauty-shopper-trends-2026/)) 
- **[Evidence]** Detail and credibility matter by platform: TikTok answers “does this work?” while Google answers “should I trust it?”, with ingredient and skincare-concern questions dominated by Google and exploratory dupe/trend questions stronger on TikTok. ([57% of beauty consumers don’t start with Google Search](https://searchengineland.com/beauty-consumers-google-search-493828)) 
- **[Assumption]** This archetype trusts concise demonstrations of longevity and ease (wear-time claims, before/after over hours, application difficulty notes) plus professional or high-volume user consensus, and discounts virality, follower count, or aesthetic packaging as proof.
- **[Assumption]** A practical social post can trigger consideration (Scenario C: viral complicated product versus reliable conventional alternative), but conversion requires corroboration in reviews/search; without it, the viral option is rejected for effort risk.

## 7. Purchase triggers and rejection criteria

- **[Evidence]** Triggers center on value and proof: price (72%) and effectiveness (55%) matter most for trying a new product, with multifunctionality influencing 15.2% of Asia-Pacific choices and long-wear/reduced reapplication driving demand for waterproof, smudge-resistant and crease-resistant formulas. ([Is Your Brand Really Worth It? Beauty Shoppers Want Proof, New Report Finds](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/is-your-brand-really-worth-it-beauty-shoppers-want-proof-new-report-finds-302921803.html)) ; ([The Art of Effortless: Simplifying Your Beauty Game](https://WWW.EUROMONITOR.COM/article/the-art-of-effortless-simplifying-your-beauty-game)) ; ([U.S. Eye Color Cosmetics Market Size & Share Report, 2033](https://www.grandviewresearch.com/industry-analysis/us-eye-color-cosmetics-market-report)) 
- **[Evidence]** Common rejection cues include inconsistent performance (uneven pigmentation, clumping, poor blending, limited wear), smudging/short wear, difficult application/removal, and irritation/sensitivity concerns. ([U.S. Eye Color Cosmetics Market Size & Share Report, 2033](https://www.grandviewresearch.com/industry-analysis/us-eye-color-cosmetics-market-report)) 
- **[Evidence]** Loyalty breaks on quality decline, price hikes without justification, poor experiences and availability problems. ([The Changing Face of Skincare Loyalty](https://www.happi.com/exclusives/the-changing-face-of-skincare-loyalty/)) 
- **[Assumption]** Purchase rule (illustrative): she buys when a product credibly promises fewer steps or longer wear without added complexity (Scenario B: one lip-and-cheek versus two separate products wins if shade, wear and application are acceptable); she rejects when use requires tutorials, frequent touch-ups, uncertain shade matching, or extensive patch-testing/guesswork.
- **[Assumption]** Stock certainty and easy fulfillment are secondary triggers: out-of-stock, overcrowded or hard-to-navigate experiences increase deferral or substitution rather than prolonged hunting.

## 8. Typical shopping decision sequence

- **[Evidence]** A common modern sequence is discovery (social, physical retail, friends) → validation (Google/search, reviews, in-store test) → comparison across retailers/products → purchase online and/or offline, rather than a linear funnel. ([57% of beauty consumers don’t start with Google Search](https://searchengineland.com/beauty-consumers-google-search-493828)) ; ([New Health & Beauty research: 5 shopper trends every marketer should know | Criteo](https://www.criteo.com/blog/5-beauty-shopper-trends-2026/)) 
- **[Evidence]** Validation is explicit: brands that make products easier to evaluate with detailed descriptions, authentic reviews and clear benefits are better positioned to convert curious shoppers. ([New Health & Beauty research: 5 shopper trends every marketer should know | Criteo](https://www.criteo.com/blog/5-beauty-shopper-trends-2026/)) 
- **[Assumption]** Archetype sequence: Identify need (low stock, routine friction, event) → screen for convenience/performance (long-wear, multifunctional, easy application) → shortlist 2–4 familiar or top-rated options → verify wear/application via reviews/demos → select reliable product → purchase via lowest-friction channel with certain availability.
- **[Assumption]** Stopping rule (modeling assumption, not a finding): she stops researching when one option has (a) consistent ratings on longevity/ease, (b) no red-flag reviews on irritation/difficult application relevant to her, and (c) acceptable price for the time saved; further searching must promise a large practical gain to continue.
- **[Assumption]** No direct evidence was found for retailer-specific replenishment features (for example, auto-replenishment or specific fulfillment options); convenient purchasing is therefore modeled generically as fast, predictable delivery/pickup and easy reordering without naming proprietary features.

## 9. Impulse purchasing versus deliberate research

- **[Evidence]** The category trend is toward more scrutiny, not less: shoppers are putting more thought into what they buy, researching and comparing before deciding what earns a place in routines. ([New Health & Beauty research: 5 shopper trends every marketer should know | Criteo](https://www.criteo.com/blog/5-beauty-shopper-trends-2026/)) 
- **[Evidence]** Overwhelm often leads to deferral rather than abandonment in beauty: those challenged by too much choice were equally likely to ultimately purchase in most categories except beauty/personal care, where they instead put the item aside with openness to buying later. ([How Having Too Much Choice Impacts Consumer Experience](https://intel.morningconsult.com/mc-content/analysis/shopper-product-choice-research)) 
- **[Evidence]** Between 30% and 40% of shoppers who had difficulty deciding cite too many products as the roadblock, and beauty/personal care is where too much choice is most likely to be an issue. ([How Having Too Much Choice Impacts Consumer Experience](https://intel.morningconsult.com/mc-content/analysis/shopper-product-choice-research)) ; 
- **[Assumption]** Impulse buying is low for this archetype: unplanned picks occur mainly as low-effort replenishment or a clearly simple add-on (mini, stick, spray, one-step hybrid), not as exploratory hauls — contrasting with trend-driven impulse and with convenience-store impulse seen in general health/beauty missions.
- **[Assumption]** Deliberate but bounded research is the norm: she will postpone rather than gamble on an uncertain product, returning later to the saved reliable option.

## 10. Factors influencing purchase confidence

- **[Evidence]** Confidence is built before checkout through reassurance: reviews/ratings, friends/family, in-store testing and search engines all play important roles. ([New Health & Beauty research: 5 shopper trends every marketer should know | Criteo](https://www.criteo.com/blog/5-beauty-shopper-trends-2026/)) 
- **[Evidence]** Familiarity plus proof reduces risk: familiar brands and customer reviews are the top two decision shortcuts when overwhelmed (50% and 48%), and brand reputation influences both trial and loyalty. ([The Changing Face of Skincare Loyalty](https://www.happi.com/exclusives/the-changing-face-of-skincare-loyalty/)) ; ([5 Factors Influencing Readers' Shopping Decisions](https://www.newbeauty.com/view/factors-influencing-readers-skin-care-shopping-decisions)) 
- **[Evidence]** Simplicity itself builds confidence: consumers want less friction to performance, with clarity, streamlined routines and fewer, more relevant recommendations increasing trust. ([Beauty Decision Fatigue Is Real - Here’s How AI Is Fixing It](https://www.revieve.com/insider/articles/beauty-decision-fatigue-ai-personalization)) ; 
- **[Evidence]** In-store friction undermines confidence: nearly 70% feel overwhelmed browsing brick-and-mortar beauty stores, with out-of-stock (47%), overcrowding (46%), long checkouts (40%) and poor navigation (34%) as top frustrations. ([Overwhelmed Shoppers Are a CX Red Flag for Beauty Retail](https://cxm.world/customer-experience/overwhelmed-shoppers-are-a-cx-red-flag-for-beauty-retail/)) ; 
- **[Assumption]** Confidence rule: clear evidence on wear duration, reapplication need, application steps/tools, and multifunctional payoff moves her to buy; vague “clean,” “premium,” or “viral” claims without time/effort specifics do not.

## 11. Evidence quality and gaps

- Overall evidence is moderate-to-strong on general beauty behavior but indirect for this exact archetype: large-sample industry surveys (Criteo N>4,500 across six markets; Stella Rising N=511; Morning Consult N=3,758; Allure N>142,000; NewBeauty N>1,300; Provoke Insights via Happi) and analyst syntheses (McKinsey 15,000+ consumers in 13 markets; Euromonitor Voice of Consumer) converge on reviews-first validation, effectiveness over novelty, convenience premiums with limits, and overwhelm-driven simplification. Publisher and dates are visible in linked pages, with most sources from 2024–2026.
- Key limitation: No direct evidence found for a defined “time-constrained professional” segment in beauty; time-scarcity effects are inferred from general convenience, simplification (skinimalism: 62% seeking simpler routines is widely cited but not independently verified here), and choice-overload studies, not from a controlled comparison of time-pressed versus non-pressed beauty shoppers.
- Willingness-to-pay boundaries are context-dependent and partly conflicting: U.S. data show both high stated willingness to pay for ease and strong price-first behavior (61% prioritize lower cost; 90% accept slower shipping), indicating the premium depends on whether the gain is reliable performance versus fulfillment speed alone — a gap requiring careful simulation rather than a single price-sensitivity value.
- Social and retail findings skew to U.S./UK/EU and to online-discoverable shoppers; in-store-only, low-digital, and non-Western routines are underrepresented, and TikTok/Reddit/YouTube anecdotes are qualitative signals only and were not treated as representative.
- No direct evidence found for retailer-specific replenishment/subscription mechanics, exact shortlist size, or minutes-spent-research thresholds for busy shoppers; those must remain explicit modeling assumptions with observable proxies (shortlist length, time to decision, touch-ups avoided) rather than cited facts.
